The Inherited Home Conversation Nobody Wants to Have — But Everybody Needs To

Jim Blackburn jamesjblackburn.com Revelations Blog

I got a call on a Tuesday afternoon from a woman named Sandra.

Her mother had passed away six weeks earlier. The family home — a three-bedroom in Coral Springs her parents had owned for 31 years — was now in her name and her brother's name, split equally through the estate. Her brother lived in Atlanta. She lived ten minutes from the house. Neither of them had any idea what to do next.

She wasn't calling because she was ready to make a decision. She was calling because she'd received three unsolicited letters from investors offering to buy the property "as-is, fast close, no hassle" — and something about those letters made her uneasy. She wanted to talk to someone who could tell her what her actual options were before she talked herself into a bad one.

That conversation lasted about forty minutes. By the end of it, she had a clear picture of five completely different paths forward — paths she didn't know existed an hour earlier.

She had options. She just needed someone to lay them out.

Why This Conversation Is So Hard

Inheriting a home is one of the most emotionally and financially complex situations a family can face — and it almost always arrives at the worst possible time.

You're grieving. You're dealing with the estate. You're navigating family dynamics that may have been simmering for years. And in the middle of all of that, there's a property with a mortgage, property taxes, utility bills, and maintenance demands that don't pause because you're not ready.

Most families avoid the financial conversation for as long as possible. That's completely understandable. But avoidance has a cost. Mortgage payments don't stop. Property taxes accrue. Carrying costs add up. And decisions that feel overwhelming in the short term become more complicated the longer they're deferred.

What I've found is that the conversation itself — the clear, calm, here-are-your-options conversation — is almost always a relief. Once people understand what's possible, the paralysis tends to lift.

What Most Families Get Wrong

Mistake #1: Assuming There Are Only Two Options

The default thinking goes like this: "we either keep it or we sell it." In reality, there are at least five or six meaningfully different paths — and the right one depends entirely on the family's financial situation, relationship dynamics, the condition of the property, and what each person actually wants going forward.

None of these options get presented in the investor letters that flood the mailbox after a death notice. Those letters have one agenda: a fast, discounted purchase. Understanding your full range of options requires a different kind of conversation.

Mistake #2: Selling Before Understanding the Tax Picture

This one costs families real money. When you inherit a property, you typically receive what's called a "stepped-up" cost basis — meaning your basis is reset to the fair market value of the home at the date of death. If your parents purchased the home in 1988 for $120,000 and it's now worth $580,000, your basis isn't $120,000. It's $580,000.

That distinction can eliminate a capital gains tax liability that would otherwise be substantial. Before any decision is made about an inherited property, have a brief conversation with a CPA. It's worth every minute.

Mistake #3: Letting Family Tension Drive a Financial Decision

Inherited properties have a way of surfacing old family dynamics alongside entirely new ones. What I've found is that having clear numbers on the table early — a professional home valuation, a breakdown of each heir's potential share under different scenarios — tends to reduce conflict significantly. When everyone is working from the same factual picture, the conversation shifts from emotional to practical.

The Five Paths Worth Knowing About

  • One heir takes the home, others are paid out. A cash-out refinance allows the staying heir to pull equity, pay off co-heirs, and take clear title.
  • The home is sold as-is. Proceeds are distributed among heirs and each person moves forward independently.
  • The home is renovated, then sold. A renovation loan can finance improvements before listing — potentially adding tens of thousands to the sale price.
  • The home becomes a rental. The income can often be used to qualify for a new home purchase — turning one inherited property into the foundation of a portfolio.
  • The payment is eliminated through a reverse mortgage. For surviving spouses aged 62 or older, a HECM can eliminate the monthly mortgage payment entirely.

Back to Sandra

Sandra and her brother ended up choosing path one. She wanted the house — she had kids in the local school district and deep roots in the neighborhood. Her brother was pragmatic and just wanted his fair share handled cleanly.

We structured a cash-out refinance based on the appraised value. Her brother was paid his share at closing. The title transferred to her name. The estate was settled.

None of that would have happened if she'd responded to one of those investor letters. She would have sold a $540,000 home for $390,000 and split the proceeds — each walking away with about $195,000 before taxes and fees. Instead, she kept a home she loved and her brother received his full fair share.

That's the difference a conversation makes.
