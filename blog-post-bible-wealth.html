Why I Think Most People Misread What the Bible Says About Wealth

Jim Blackburn jamesjblackburn.com Revelations Blog

I've spent twenty-five years in the mortgage industry. I've sat across the table from people buying their first home and people refinancing their fourth. I've helped families build wealth and watched families lose everything. I know what money does to people — the good and the bad.

I've also spent twenty-five years studying what the Bible actually says. Not what I was told it says. Not what the pastor quoted on Sunday morning with the offering plate making its rounds. What the text actually says when you sit with it, in the original languages, without an institution standing between you and the page.

And here's what I've found: almost everyone misreads what the Bible says about wealth. But not in the way you'd expect.

The Two Misreadings

There are two dominant misreadings, and they come from opposite directions.

The first misreading says wealth is sinful. Money is the root of all evil. The rich man can't enter heaven. If you have too much, God is disappointed in you. Give it away. Feel guilty. Apologize for success.

The second misreading says wealth is a blessing. God wants you to prosper. Name it and claim it. Your bank account is a measure of your faith. If you're broke, you're not believing hard enough.

Both are wrong. And both serve the institution more than the individual.

The guilt version keeps you dependent. If wealth is sinful, then the institution becomes the safe place to deposit your money — tithes, offerings, building funds. You hand it over because keeping it feels spiritually dangerous. The institution benefits. You stay small.

The prosperity version keeps you chasing. If wealth proves God's favor, then the institution becomes the pipeline to that favor — sow a seed, give to receive, unlock your blessing through bigger and bigger donations. The institution benefits. You stay hungry.

Same result. Different packaging. Both versions turn money into a spiritual test that only the institution can grade.

What the Text Actually Says

The verse everyone quotes is 1 Timothy 6:10. "Money is the root of all evil." Except that's not what it says.

It says: the love of money is the root of all kinds of evil. Three words change everything. "The love of." And "all kinds of" — not all evil, period. Some kinds. When money becomes the thing you love most — when it replaces connection, replaces purpose, replaces people — that's when it corrodes.

Money itself is neutral. It's a tool. Like a hammer. You can build a house with it or you can break a window. The hammer doesn't care. The question is always about the hand holding it.

When Jesus told the rich young ruler to sell everything, he wasn't making a universal rule about wealth. He was diagnosing one man's specific attachment. That man's wealth was the wall between him and the life he was being invited into. For someone else, the wall might be pride, or fear, or a relationship they can't let go of. Jesus wasn't anti-wealth. He was anti-wall.

The Toll Booth Problem

Here's where my reading diverges from most of what you'll hear in church.

I believe the Bible contains two kinds of truth. Discovered truth — truth that exists whether anyone teaches it or not — and inherited truth — truth that exists only because an institution told you it was true and you believed them.

"Love your neighbor as yourself" is discovered truth. You don't need a priest to tell you it's real. You feel it. Every culture on earth arrived at some version of it independently. It works whether you read it in Leviticus or hear it from your grandmother.

But "bring your tithes to the storehouse or God will curse your finances"? That's inherited truth. It serves the storehouse. Ask yourself: who benefits from that teaching? The person giving — or the institution receiving?

I call this the toll booth problem. Someone built a toll booth on a road that was always free. And they convinced you that paying the toll was the only way to reach God. It's not. The road was free before they arrived. It's free after they leave. They just need you to believe you can't walk it without them.

What I've Learned From Both Sides of the Table

In the mortgage business, I've watched money reveal people. Not change them — reveal them. The generous person who gets wealthy becomes more generous. The fearful person who gets wealthy becomes more controlling. Money is an amplifier. It turns up the volume on whatever's already playing.

The Bible says the same thing, if you read it honestly. Abraham was wealthy. Job was wealthy. Solomon was the richest man alive. David was a king. These weren't cautionary tales about the dangers of success. They were stories about what people did with what they had. Some built temples. Some wrote psalms that still make people weep three thousand years later.

The money wasn't the variable. The heart was.

The Question Nobody Asks

Every Sunday, someone stands at a pulpit and talks about money. Tithing sermons. Stewardship campaigns. Capital improvement drives. And the question that's never asked — the question the institution hopes you'll never think to ask — is this:

Does this teaching open a door or close one?

A teaching that says "use what you have to connect with people, to serve, to build, to heal" — that opens a door. It sends you outward. It makes you more generous, more creative, more alive.

A teaching that says "give your money to us or God will be displeased" — that closes a door. It pulls you inward. It makes you anxious, obligated, dependent.

The first sounds like discovered truth. The second sounds like a toll booth.

Where I've Landed

After twenty-five years in finance and twenty-five years in scripture, here's where I've landed:

Money is not good or evil. It's a mirror. It shows you what you already are. If you're connected to the people around you — if you see them, serve them, love them — money becomes a tool for more of that. If you're disconnected — if money is the thing you hold instead of holding people — then it becomes the wall Jesus was talking about.

The Bible doesn't condemn wealth. The Bible condemns walls. Anything that goes between you and the people you're meant to love — money, power, ideology, institution — that's the problem. Not the dollar amount. The distance it creates.

The road is still free. Walk it. Build something. Give generously — but give because your heart is full, not because someone told you the roof needs fixing. The door was never locked.
