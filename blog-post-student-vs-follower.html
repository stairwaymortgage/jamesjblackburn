The Difference Between Being a Student of Jesus and a Follower of Christ

Jim Blackburn jamesjblackburn.com Revelations Blog

I grew up in church. Sang the songs. Said the prayers. Did the Sunday school worksheets with the fill-in-the-blank answers about David and Goliath. I was a good Christian kid, which mostly meant I could recite the books of the Bible in order and I didn't swear until I was fifteen.

But somewhere in my twenties, a question started itching at me that nobody in my church could scratch. And it was this:

Are we worshipping the window — or walking through it?

That question took me twenty-five years to answer. I studied world religions. I read the original languages. I sat with the texts — not the translations, not the commentaries, not the sermon notes — the texts. I filled five thousand notes in Evernote trying to understand what I was actually looking at.

And what I found was a distinction that changed everything. A distinction the institution never taught me, because teaching it would make the institution unnecessary.

A Student of Jesus Studies the Man

Being a student of Jesus means you study what he said, where he went, what he did, and what happened to him. You learn the parables. You memorize the Beatitudes. You debate whether he literally walked on water or whether that's metaphor. You argue about the resurrection. You read scholars who argue about scholars who argue about manuscripts.

This is valuable. I'm not dismissing it. Scholarship matters. History matters. Understanding the context in which a man stood up against an empire and said "the kingdom of God is within you" — that matters enormously.

But studying Jesus can become its own kind of trap. Because you can study a man your entire life and never do what he did. You can write a dissertation on his teachings and never live one of them. You can become the world's foremost expert on the Sermon on the Mount and still treat your neighbor like a stranger.

A student of Jesus knows what he said. That's knowledge. And knowledge is not what he was offering.

A Follower of Christ Walks the Path He Demonstrated

Here's where the distinction gets sharp.

The word Christ is not Jesus's last name. It's a title. It comes from the Greek Christos, which translates the Hebrew Mashiach — "the anointed one." It describes a state of being, not a surname. It describes someone who has been touched by something beyond ordinary human experience and is now operating from that place.

What Jesus demonstrated — more completely than any human being before or since — was what happens when a person learns to detach from their small, temporal identity and live from something larger. He called it the Father. He called it the kingdom within. He called it the I AM.

When he said "I am the way, the truth, and the life," the institution heard: I, Jesus, am the only way. Worship me. But that's not what the words say. What if the emphasis is on I AM?

The same I AM that spoke from the burning bush. The same I AM that lives in every human consciousness. The way is not a man. The way is the state of awareness that the man was living from.

A follower of Christ doesn't just study what Jesus said. A follower of Christ tries to access the same state he accessed. Not to worship the window — but to walk through it. Not to make him a god above you, but to accept his invitation to stand beside him.

He never said: Worship me. I'm above you. Build a building in my name and charge people to enter it.

He said: Follow me. Do what I did. Love what I loved. The things I do, you will do also — and greater things than these.

Read that again. Greater things than these. He wasn't building a ceiling. He was opening a door.

Why the Institution Prefers Students

The institution — any institution, in any religion, across all of history — prefers students to followers. Students need teachers. Students need classrooms. Students need credentialed authorities to tell them what the text means. Students create demand for the institution.

Followers don't need any of that. Followers need a path and the willingness to walk it. Followers are dangerous to institutions because they eventually realize the door was never locked. They realize they don't need a mediator between themselves and God. They realize the toll booth was charging for a road that was always free.

So the institution does what institutions do. It takes the most revolutionary spiritual teacher in human history — a man who literally overturned tables in the temple to protest the monetization of access to God — and turns him into a product. Builds a building. Hires staff. Creates a curriculum. Charges admission. Sells the very thing he died to give away for free.

He didn't die so you'd need the church. He died because the church needed him gone.

The Sorting

I'm not anti-church. I'm not anti-Bible. I'm not trying to tear anything down. I'm trying to sort.

The Bible contains two kinds of material. There are verses that open doors — that invite you in, that widen the circle, that make you feel the weight of being loved without condition. And there are verses that close doors — that create hierarchy, that establish insiders and outsiders, that make you feel small so you'll need someone to make you feel big again.

The door-opening verses show up in every religion on earth. Every culture. Every grandmother's kitchen. "Love your neighbor." "Do unto others." "You are not alone." These are discovered truths — they exist whether anyone teaches them or not.

The door-closing verses tend to show up in one tradition, debated even within that tradition, and they always — always — benefit the people teaching them more than the people hearing them.

Jesus sorted. He healed on the Sabbath when the law said don't. He touched lepers when the law said don't. He ate with sinners when the system said don't. And he said the most radical thing anyone has ever said in a religious context: "You have heard it said… but I say unto you." He was sorting scripture in public. And he was killed for it.

If he sorted, we're allowed to sort.

Where I've Landed

I am not a student of Jesus. I was, for a long time. I studied the man. I studied the history. I studied the arguments about the arguments.

Now I'm trying to be a follower of Christ. Which means I'm trying to access what he accessed. I'm trying to live from the I AM instead of from my ego. I'm trying to love without condition, serve without transaction, and stand in truth even when it costs something.

I fail at this daily. Hourly. But the path is clear. And the path doesn't run through an institution. It runs through the space between you and the person sitting next to you. The thread that connects. The connection that is God — not a being sitting on a throne somewhere beyond the sky, but the living, breathing, unbreakable bond between every soul that has ever existed.

That's what he taught. That's what he demonstrated. That's what he died for.

The door was never locked. But someone convinced you it was, and then charged you for the key.

Put the key down. Try the door. It opens. It always opened.
